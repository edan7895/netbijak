Broadband Speed Test Result

✨ NetBijak AI Summary

Understanding your broadband speed test results is essential for determining if your internet connection is performing as expected. While MCMC sets a 90% throughput benchmark for fibre services, many factors like hardware limitations, distance from your router, and network congestion can influence your actual speeds. It is important to distinguish between wired and wireless results, as WiFi performance is naturally affected by your home environment. A consistent reading near 90-95Mbps on a faster plan often indicates a hardware bottleneck, such as an outdated cable or router port, rather than an issue with your ISP. By conducting controlled tests and verifying your equipment, you can identify whether your service requires troubleshooting or if your hardware simply needs an upgrade. Always remember to consider the difference between Mbps and MB/s when evaluating download speeds.

How to Understand Your Broadband Speed Test Result

A speed test result only becomes useful when you know what number is normal for your broadband plan and what a low result actually indicates.

Seeing 420Mbps on a 500Mbps plan is very different from seeing 90Mbps. A 90Mbps result on a 300Mbps connection may point to a hardware bottleneck, while a lower WiFi result may simply reflect wireless conditions.

The first step is to compare the result with your subscribed speed.

What Speed Should You Get From Your Broadband Plan?

A speed test will not necessarily show the exact number printed on your broadband package.

For Fibre broadband, MCMC's wired broadband service standard uses at least 90% of subscribed speed for at least 90% of the time as a throughput benchmark. This is a service-quality reference, not a promise that every individual speed test will always show 90% or more.

Broadband Plan90% Reference
100Mbps90Mbps
300Mbps270Mbps
500Mbps450Mbps
1Gbps900Mbps
2Gbps1.8Gbps

So, for example, a properly conducted wired test of 480Mbps on a 500Mbps plan is broadly in line with the 90% reference.

A result of 250Mbps on the same plan is a different situation. It is well below the reference and deserves further investigation.

Source: MCMC – Industry Performance Report

How to Read Your Result

Rather than asking whether your test result matches the advertised speed exactly, look at how close it is to your subscribed speed and whether the result is consistent.

For example:

500Mbps plan → 480Mbps

This is around 96% of the subscribed speed and is within the 90% reference.

500Mbps plan → 420Mbps

This is around 84%. It is below the 90% reference, so repeated results under controlled conditions are worth checking.

500Mbps plan → 90Mbps

This is only 18% of the subscribed speed. A result this low should immediately prompt a check for a 100Mbps connection bottleneck or another obvious limitation before blaming the Fibre line.

The pattern matters more than one isolated number.

Why Is Your Speed Test Lower Than the Plan?

A lower result does not automatically mean your ISP is delivering an inadequate Fibre connection.

The result can be affected by several parts of the connection between the Fibre line and your device.

WiFi

WiFi performance depends on factors such as distance from the router, walls, interference, wireless congestion and the capability of the connected device.

A 500Mbps Fibre plan might produce a much lower result in a bedroom than beside the router.

That does not automatically mean the Fibre connection itself is slow.

Router

The router's hardware and Ethernet ports can become a bottleneck.

An older router or a LAN port limited to 100Mbps cannot properly pass a faster wired Fibre connection to your computer.

Ethernet Connection

A wired connection can still be limited if the computer, adapter, switch or cable is only negotiating at 100Mbps.

This is particularly important when testing plans above 100Mbps.

Your Device

A phone, laptop or desktop may not have hardware capable of reaching the full speed of the broadband package.

The result therefore reflects the complete connection path, not just the Fibre line.

Other Network Traffic

Cloud backups, downloads, video streaming, game updates and other connected devices can consume bandwidth during the test and reduce the number you see.


Why Is My 300Mbps Plan Only Getting 90–95Mbps?

A repeated result around 90–95Mbps on a 300Mbps, 500Mbps or 1Gbps plan is a particularly useful warning sign.

The number is suspiciously close to the practical limit of a 100Mbps Ethernet connection.

Possible causes include:

  1. A 100Mbps Ethernet port
  2. A 100Mbps network adapter
  3. A Fast Ethernet switch
  4. An older router LAN port
  5. A damaged or unsuitable Ethernet connection

For example, if a 300Mbps plan repeatedly produces around 94Mbps over a wired connection, the problem may be that the computer and router are communicating at 100Mbps.

Changing from 300Mbps to 500Mbps will not solve that problem.

The hardware link must support Gigabit Ethernet first.


WiFi Result vs Wired Result: Which One Matters?

The answer depends on what you are trying to find out.

A wired result is more useful for assessing whether the broadband connection itself is performing properly.

A WiFi result is more useful for understanding the speed a particular device receives in your home.

For example:

TestResultWhat It Suggests
500Mbps Fibre – LAN480MbpsFibre connection is performing well
500Mbps Fibre – WiFi near router350MbpsWireless result is lower but may be normal
500Mbps Fibre – WiFi in distant room120MbpsHome WiFi conditions may be the limiting factor
500Mbps Fibre – LAN92MbpsCheck for a 100Mbps hardware bottleneck

This is why a phone showing 120Mbps does not by itself prove that a 500Mbps Fibre service is underperforming.


Mbps vs MB/s: Why Does Your Download Look Slower?

Broadband packages are normally advertised in Mbps, or megabits per second.

Some download applications instead display MB/s, or megabytes per second.

There are 8 bits in 1 byte, so divide the Mbps figure by 8 to get the theoretical equivalent in MB/s.

Broadband SpeedTheoretical Conversion
100Mbps12.5MB/s
300Mbps37.5MB/s
500Mbps62.5MB/s
1Gbps125MB/s
2Gbps250MB/s

These are mathematical conversions, not guaranteed real-world download rates.

Actual file-download speeds can be lower because of protocol overhead, server limitations, network conditions and the device's own performance.

So a download running at 35MB/s on a 300Mbps connection is roughly equivalent to 280Mbps, not 35Mbps.


What Does a Very Low Speed Test Result Mean?

The size of the gap matters.

Around 90–95Mbps on a faster Fibre plan

Check for a 100Mbps connection limit first.

Around 70–85% of the subscribed speed

This is below the 90% reference and is worth monitoring with repeated, controlled tests. Do not immediately assume the ISP is at fault.

Far below the subscribed speed

For example, a 500Mbps plan repeatedly showing 200Mbps on a properly configured wired test is more significant than a result of 450Mbps versus 500Mbps.

The further the result is from the expected level, the more useful it is to investigate the connection path.

One unusually low result

One bad test is weak evidence by itself.

Speed tests are snapshots of network performance. A single result can be affected by temporary network conditions, background traffic or the test server.

The pattern across multiple tests is more useful.


When Is a Speed Test Result Actually a Problem?

A low result becomes much more meaningful when all of the following are true:

The test is wired

The Ethernet connection supports the subscribed speed

Background traffic has been stopped

The result is consistently low

The result remains significantly below the expected level

At that point, the issue is more likely to justify technical investigation by your ISP.

Keep your test screenshots and record the testing time. A series of consistent wired results is much stronger evidence than saying that your internet “feels slow”.


Should You Upgrade Your Broadband Because of a Low Speed Test?

Not immediately.

If your 300Mbps plan is only producing 90Mbps, upgrading to 500Mbps does not fix the underlying bottleneck if the computer is still connected through a 100Mbps interface.

Likewise, if a 500Mbps wired test is already producing around 470–500Mbps but your bedroom WiFi only reaches 100Mbps, buying a faster Fibre package may not improve the bedroom result.

The first question should be:

Where is the bottleneck?

It could be the Fibre service, the router, the Ethernet link, the WiFi network or the device.

Only after identifying that bottleneck does a broadband upgrade make sense.


NetBijak's Take

A speed test number should be interpreted, not simply compared with the headline speed on your broadband bill.

For Fibre, the 90% reference provides a practical benchmark: 300Mbps → 270Mbps, 500Mbps → 450Mbps, and 1Gbps → 900Mbps. MCMC's standard applies the throughput benchmark over time, so one isolated test should not be treated as proof of an ISP performance failure.

The most useful warning sign is often not a small difference from the advertised speed, but an obvious pattern such as a 300Mbps plan repeatedly producing 90–95Mbps.

That points you toward the network path and possible 100Mbps limitations before you start blaming the Fibre service.

Use the result to answer the right question:

Is the Fibre connection slow, or is something in my home network preventing me from seeing the speed I am paying for?
