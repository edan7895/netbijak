Can an ISP Change Your Monthly Rate During a 24-Month Contract?

✨ NetBijak AI Summary

Many Malaysian broadband users assume that a 24-month contract locks in their monthly fee, but this is not always guaranteed. While ISPs often include clauses that allow them to revise pricing, they must still follow the General Consumer Code of Practice. This includes providing at least 15 working days' notice for significant price increases and offering the option to terminate the contract without penalties. It is important to distinguish between new-customer promotions, which are not automatically available to you, and actual base-plan price changes. If your bill increases, you should verify the specific contract clauses, the notice period provided, and your right to terminate under consumer protection laws. Always review your original service agreement carefully before challenging a price hike with your provider.

A 24-month broadband contract does not automatically mean the monthly price can never change.

At the same time, an ISP cannot treat the contract as a blank cheque to change your charges without following the applicable contract terms and consumer-protection requirements.

For Malaysian broadband users, three questions matter:

Can the ISP increase the price? Can existing customers get a lower new price? What can you do if the increase is disputed?

Can an ISP Raise Your Price During the Contract?

The first document to check is your signed Terms & Conditions.

For example, Maxis' current Home Fibre terms, version 22 September 2026, expressly state that Maxis reserves the right to revise its service and pricing. The same terms provide for 24- or 36-month commitment periods.

However, the ISP's contractual power is not the end of the question.

The General Consumer Code of Practice (GCC) for Malaysia's communications and multimedia industry requires the contract to explain the procedure for changing its terms. Where a permitted variation results in an increase in charges or a substantial adverse impact on the service, the provider must give affected subscribers at least 15 working days' prior written notice.

The same GCC says the subscriber must be allowed to terminate the contract before the change takes effect without termination charges, apart from incidental costs, where the change increases charges or causes a substantial adverse impact that the subscriber can demonstrate.

The GCC is a registered consumer-protection code for Malaysia's communications and multimedia industry and operates alongside relevant legislation including the Consumer Protection Act 1999.

So the practical answer is:

A mid-contract price increase may be possible, but it is not simply “the ISP can raise anything at any time”.

Does a 24-Month Contract Guarantee the Price?

Not necessarily.

This is where many consumers misunderstand the word “contract”.

A 24-month commitment normally tells you how long you are committed to the service and what happens if you terminate early. It does not, by itself, prove that the monthly fee is frozen for the entire period.

For example, Maxis' current Home Fibre terms provide a 24-month commitment and an early termination formula based on the monthly charge multiplied by the remaining months. At the same time, the same document reserves the provider's right to revise pricing.

That is why you need to read both the commitment clause and the price-variation clause.

What If the ISP Lowers the Price for New Customers?

This is a different issue.

A new promotional price does not automatically become your new monthly rate simply because you are already subscribed to the same broadband speed.

The GCC requires providers to clearly disclose service charges and identify charges that may vary during the contract, but it does not say that every later promotion must automatically be extended or backdated to existing subscribers.

Providers can set eligibility conditions for promotions.

For example, TIME currently offers an RM20 monthly discount to eligible existing 600Mbps customers, but the offer requires the customer to meet the stated eligibility criteria and renew for another 12 months.

That means:

New advertised price ≠ automatic price match for existing customers.

The promotion's own terms determine who qualifies.

What If the ISP Actually Cuts the Price of the Existing Plan?

That is more interesting.

If the provider announces that the existing plan itself has been reduced for all subscribers, the position is different from a new-customer promotion. You should check exactly how the provider describes the change.

There are also cases where an ISP gives existing customers better value without changing their original monthly fee.

For example, Maxis' current legacy-plan speed upgrade states that eligible customers can receive a higher speed while their monthly fee remains unchanged, and customers under contract keep the existing contract period.

So when comparing your old plan with a new offer, check whether it is:

A lower public price for new sign-ups

or

A genuine price reduction applied to existing subscribers.

They are not the same thing.

What Does the Consumer Protection Act Say?

Malaysia's Consumer Protection Act 1999 contains provisions on unfair contract terms.

Section 24D says a contract term may be substantively unfair in circumstances including where it is harsh, oppressive or unconscionable. The Act also specifically lists a term that allows the supplier to modify the contract unilaterally as one of the circumstances a court or Tribunal may consider when assessing substantive unfairness.

This does not mean every unilateral price-change clause is automatically void.

The court or Tribunal has to consider the circumstances and the wording of the contract. Where a term is found to be procedurally or substantively unfair, the Act allows the court or Tribunal to declare the term or contract unenforceable or void.

That distinction is important:

“The ISP increased my bill” does not automatically mean “the ISP broke the law.”

The stronger complaint is:

“The increase conflicts with the contractual terms, the required notice process, or applicable consumer-protection requirements.”

What Should You Challenge When the Price Goes Up?

Do not start by arguing that “24 months means fixed price”.

Ask the ISP for four specific things:

1. The exact contract clause allowing the increase

Ask them to identify the clause in the T&C that permits the change.

2. The effective date

Check whether the required advance notice was actually provided.

3. The reason for the increase

Find out whether this is a base subscription-price increase, a tax or government charge, an add-on, or a change to the service itself.

4. Your termination rights

If the change falls within GCC clause 4.5, ask whether you are entitled to terminate without the usual early termination charge.

Keep the original sign-up confirmation, contract, promotion screenshot, invoices and the ISP's price-change notice.

What If the ISP Refuses?

Make the complaint in writing first.

If the provider does not resolve the dispute, CFM currently accepts complaints involving Bill & Charge, Unfair Practice, Terms & Conditions Disputes and Plan or Package Pricing. Complaints can be submitted through the MCMC Consumer Redress Portal, and CFM provides the complaint service free of charge.

That gives you a much stronger record than arguing with a customer-service agent over the phone without keeping any documentation.

What About an ISP Offering a Lower Price to Someone Else?

You generally need to distinguish between price discrimination that is part of a legitimate promotion and a situation where the provider has changed the contractual price that applies to you.

An ISP can publish promotions with specific eligibility conditions. TIME's current RM20 existing-customer promotion, for example, is limited to customers meeting defined conditions rather than automatically applying to every 600Mbps subscriber.

So the useful comparison is not:

“My neighbour pays less.”

It is:

“Does the promotion's eligibility include my existing subscription, and does my contract or the provider's announcement say existing customers receive the same rate?”

NetBijak's Take

A 24-month Fibre contract does not by itself guarantee that the monthly rate can never change, but an ISP's ability to change charges is constrained by its contract and the applicable consumer framework. The GCC requires advance notice for qualifying price increases and provides a charge-free termination route in specified circumstances; meanwhile, a cheaper new-customer promotion does not automatically give existing users a right to the same price.
