Fibre Cable Cut by Roadworks: How Infrastructure Damage Gets Repaired

✨ NetBijak AI Summary

When roadworks accidentally sever an underground fibre optic cable, the resulting internet outage often affects an entire neighborhood rather than a single home. This happens because high-capacity feeder cables carry traffic for thousands of users before splitting into individual residential lines. Repairing these breaks is a complex process that involves locating the fault, ensuring site safety, and meticulously splicing together many strands of glass fibre. Sometimes, ISPs can temporarily restore service by rerouting traffic through alternative paths, but this depends on existing network capacity and design. Consequently, physical repairs can take several days due to the challenges of excavation and the delicate nature of fibre optic infrastructure. Understanding this process helps clarify why service restoration isn't always as simple as joining two cut cables.

A road contractor accidentally cuts an underground fibre cable. Suddenly, hundreds or thousands of Unifi, Maxis or other broadband users in the surrounding area lose service.

This can happen because the damaged cable is not a single household fibre. It may be part of the feeder or distribution network carrying traffic towards multiple homes and network access points. In a typical FTTH network, feeder fibre runs from the provider's central office towards distribution points, where the network is split towards individual customers. (Corning; FOA)

Why One Cable Cut Can Affect a Whole Area

A residential fibre network is not built as one independent cable from every house all the way back to the ISP.

A typical PON architecture looks roughly like this:

ISP / OLT
│
Feeder / Main Fibre
│
Distribution Point / Splitter
│
Distribution Fibre
├── House A
├── House B
├── House C
├── House D
└── ...

The feeder cable can carry fibres serving multiple downstream distribution points. Corning describes feeder, distribution and drop sections as separate parts of a typical FTTH network, with the feeder connecting the central office to the distribution network. (Corning)

So when roadworks cut a strategically important section, the effect can extend far beyond the property directly beside the excavation.

What Happens After the Cable Is Cut?

The repair does not start with someone immediately joining the two visible cable ends.

The network team first needs to determine where the break is and what services are affected.

A typical response looks like:

1. Detect the fault

Network monitoring or customer reports identify the affected area.

2. Locate the physical break

Technicians use fibre testing and network records to determine the damaged cable section.

3. Assess whether traffic can be rerouted

If another network path is available, traffic may be moved away from the damaged section.

TM has previously documented this exact approach after fibre cables were cut during construction work in Sentul and Sabak Bernam. TM said its technical team started repair work while it simultaneously optimised the network by rerouting traffic, allowing affected services to be restored before the physical cable repair and replacement work had finished. (TM)

That distinction is important:

Internet service restored ≠ damaged fibre physically repaired.

Why Roadworks Make the Repair More Complicated

If the damaged cable is underground, the technician cannot simply stand beside the road and splice it.

The damaged section may be inside:

  1. Underground ducts
  2. Manholes
  3. Handholes
  4. Cable chambers
  5. Road reserves

The repair team may need safe access to the cable before the actual fibre work can begin.

Malaysia's JKR utility procedures specifically incorporate Underground Utility Detection Mapping (UDM) and survey requirements for road projects, recognising the need to identify underground utilities before construction work. (JKR)

This is also why a damaged fibre cable can become a roadwork problem rather than simply a telecommunications technician job.

What Happens to the Cut Fibre?

Once the damaged section is accessible, the network team determines whether the cable can be repaired using a splice or whether a section of cable needs to be replaced.

Large FTTH networks contain multiple fibre strands and use splice closures and distribution equipment at different points. CommScope's FTTH architecture documentation shows feeder fibres being spliced into distribution and splitter infrastructure at network closures. (CommScope)

The repair therefore has to reconnect the correct fibres, not simply “join the cable”.

The technician must identify the affected fibres, prepare the cable, splice them and protect the completed splice inside an appropriate closure.

Why a Main Trunk Repair Can Take Days

The actual fibre splice is not necessarily the slowest part.

The difficult part can be everything around it:

Finding the exact break

A road excavation can expose only part of the network, and the visible damage may not tell the technician which fibres and services are affected.

Getting access to the cable

If the damaged section is underneath an active road, pavement or construction zone, safe excavation and traffic control may be required.

Handling a high-fibre-count cable

A backbone or feeder cable can contain many fibres serving downstream network equipment. Repairing it requires accurate identification and restoration of the affected circuits.

Restoring network configuration

If traffic was temporarily rerouted, the network may need to be returned to its normal path after the physical repair.

Testing

The repaired fibre must be tested before the network is considered stable.

This is why a major cable cut can take substantially longer than repairing a single customer's drop cable.

Why Unifi and Maxis Can Both Go Down

This depends on how the providers' physical networks are built in that location.

Different broadband brands do not necessarily mean completely separate physical routes. A provider may use shared infrastructure, wholesale access arrangements or facilities located along the same corridor.

Therefore, one piece of damaged infrastructure can potentially affect multiple services.

The important distinction is:

“Unifi and Maxis are different brands”

does not automatically mean:

“Unifi and Maxis have completely independent physical fibre routes everywhere.”

A common construction corridor, shared duct or other infrastructure dependency can create a common point of failure.

Can the ISP Bypass the Damage?

Sometimes.

A provider with an alternative route may reroute traffic around the failed section.

TM has documented a fibre-cut incident where rerouting was used to restore affected services while cable repair and replacement continued. (TM)

But rerouting depends on the actual network design and available capacity.

There is no guarantee that every residential area has enough spare paths to keep all services running after a major trunk failure.

What Customers Should Check During a Major Fibre Outage

When many homes in the same area suddenly lose broadband, check the provider's network-status page before repeatedly rebooting your ONT.

Unifi directs customers to check for area-wide maintenance or network outages before starting individual troubleshooting. (Unifi)

Maxis likewise provides an address-based network checker for planned maintenance and unexpected service disruptions affecting Fibre and mobile services. (Maxis)

If multiple neighbours using the same or different providers lose service at roughly the same time, an infrastructure-level fault becomes more plausible than multiple independent home-router failures.

What About Service Restoration Guarantees?

Don't assume a major roadworks fibre cut will be repaired within the same timeframe as a normal home fault.

For example, Unifi's published 24-hour Service Restoration programme states that eligible customers can receive a RM50 bill rebate when service is not restored within 24 hours after an official complaint, subject to its stated conditions and exclusions. (Unifi)

A large infrastructure failure can involve circumstances beyond a standard in-home fault, so the provider's applicable service terms and outage conditions matter.

NetBijak's Take

A roadwork-related fibre cut becomes a serious outage when the damaged cable is part of the feeder or trunk network serving multiple downstream areas. The repair involves fault localisation, access to the underground cable, fibre identification, splicing or cable replacement and network testing; traffic may be rerouted first, but physical restoration can continue afterwards.
